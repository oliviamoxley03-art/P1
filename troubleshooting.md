# Troubleshooting: Common Issues With This Lesson

Here's what tends to come up when teaching this lesson, based on classroom experience with Level 1 and 2 EML students.

## "Students used casual or spoken English."

**What it looks like:** Sentences like "Hey, I need my grades" or "Can u help me with this."

**Why it happens:** Spoken, conversational English is what students have practiced the most, so it comes out first, especially under time pressure.

**What to do:** Instead of just marking it wrong, put a casual sentence next to its formal version on the board ("Can u help me" → "I would like to request your assistance with..."). Keep a running "casual vs. formal" anchor chart in the room students can reference later.

## "Students copied the sentence starters word-for-word."

**What it looks like:** Every letter reads almost the same, with only the topic swapped out.

**Why it happens:** This is expected at Level 1 and 2. The point of this lesson is correct genre conventions, not originality. It only becomes a concern as students move toward Level 3 or 4.

**What to do:** For higher-level students, require at least one sentence that doesn't use a provided starter, to push them toward building formal sentences independently.

## "Students got the parts of the letter in the wrong order."

**What it looks like:** No salutation, the closing appears before the body, or the date is missing entirely.

**Why it happens:** This is a structural issue, not a language issue, and it happens even to students who can write accurate individual sentences.

**What to do:** Return to the sorting activity (Step 3) as a quick warm-up before drafting, instead of moving straight into independent writing.

## "Students didn't know who they were supposed to sound like."

**What it looks like:** A student stops halfway through and asks, "Wait, who am I writing this to?" or writes the letter as if talking to a friend or a teacher.

**Why it happens:** Business letters assume an audience the student doesn't know personally. Students used to writing for someone familiar don't always think to shift their tone for someone they've never met.

**What to do:** Before drafting, have students name their recipient and answer one question out loud with a partner: "Does this person know you? What do they need to know to help you?" This only takes a minute and can happen right before Step 5.

## "A student's home language doesn't mark formality the way English does."

**What it looks like:** A student is genuinely confused by the idea that "polite" and "formal" require different sentence structures, not just different words.

**Why it happens:** Not every language marks formality through separate grammar or heavy indirect phrasing. Some rely more on word choice or context instead.

**What to do:** Name the difference directly instead of treating it as a gap to fix. Something like, "In some languages this works differently. In English business writing, indirect phrases like 'Would it be possible to...' are often used to sound polite." Naming it usually works better than repeated correction.

## One More Thing

Most of these problems are prevented, not fixed, by keeping Steps 2 and 3 in the lesson instead of cutting them to save time. Even Level 3 and 4 students benefit from seeing the structure isolated before writing it themselves.
